Essential Financial Ratios for Long-Term Investors — A Complete Plain-Language Guide

Sarat Rout May 9, 2026 18 min read Fundamental Analysis
Long-term investors rely on financial ratios — not market noise — to identify businesses worth owning for years. Understanding what each ratio measures, and what it hides, is the foundation of disciplined stock analysis.

Financial ratios are the investor's toolkit for cutting through the noise of market prices and quarterly headlines to understand what a business actually is — how profitable it is, how efficiently it uses capital, how liquid it stays, and whether its stock is reasonably priced relative to what it earns and owns. This guide covers all the key ratios you'll encounter in a stock screener, grouped by what they measure, with plain-language explanations, formulas, examples, and most importantly — what to watch for as a long-term investor.

How to Use This Guide

No single ratio tells the full story. Ratios must be read together — a high P/E with a high PEG and falling ROCE is very different from a high P/E with a low PEG and rising ROCE. Compare ratios against the company's own history and the sector average — what is acceptable varies significantly by industry.

This guide uses the same ratio labels you see in screener columns. Each ratio is explained with its formula, what to look for in isolation, and — most importantly — what matters for long-term investing.

Valuation Ratios

How much are you paying for the business relative to its earnings, assets, and sales?

P/E Ratio and Relative P/E

The Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio is the most widely used valuation metric. It tells you how many rupees an investor is willing to pay for every rupee of current annual earnings.

Formula

P/E = Current Market Price (CMP) ÷ Earnings Per Share (EPS)

Interpretation

  • P/E = 20: Investors pay ₹20 for every ₹1 of current earnings
  • Low P/E: Potentially undervalued — or the market expects poor growth
  • High P/E: Expensive — or the market expects strong future growth
  • Negative P/E: Company is making losses — P/E is meaningless

Relative P/E (Sector)

Relative P/E compares the company's P/E to the sector average P/E. A ratio above 1.0 means the company trades at a premium to its peers; below 1.0 means a discount. Useful for identifying which companies within a sector are expensive or cheap relative to peers.

P/E Alone Can Mislead

A low P/E can mean a company is cheap — or that it has poor growth, falling earnings, or high debt. A high P/E can mean overvalued — or that the company has outstanding growth ahead. Always pair P/E with growth (PEG), quality (ROCE), and debt (Debt/Equity).

PEG Ratio — Price/Earnings to Growth

The PEG Ratio improves on the P/E by factoring in the company's earnings growth rate. A high P/E is acceptable if growth is fast enough — PEG puts this in perspective. Popularised by legendary investor Peter Lynch.

Formula

PEG = P/E Ratio ÷ Earnings Growth Rate (%)

PEG Interpretation
PEG < 1
Undervalued relative to growth. Stock is cheap compared to its earnings growth. Often the best sweet spot for long-term investors.
PEG = 1
Fairly valued. The stock price exactly reflects its growth rate. Reasonable entry for quality companies.
PEG 1 – 2
Slightly expensive. Growth expected to justify the premium. Acceptable for high-quality compounders with durable moats.
PEG > 2
Overvalued. You are paying heavily for growth that may not materialise. High risk of disappointment if growth slows.
PEG < 0
Negative / Meaningless. Earnings are declining (negative growth rate). PEG loses its interpretive value. Company is shrinking — high risk.
Best Range for Investment

Target companies with PEG between 0.5 and 1.0 — undervalued relative to growth. Between 1.0 and 2.0 can be acceptable for exceptional quality businesses. Avoid negative PEG (declining earnings) and PEG above 3.0 unless you have very strong conviction on future growth acceleration.

EV/EBITDA and M.Cap/Sales

EV / EBITDA

Enterprise Value to EBITDA compares a company's total value (including debt) to its earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortisation. It is capital-structure neutral — useful for comparing companies with different debt levels.

Formulas

EV = Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash
EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value ÷ EBITDA

EV/EBITDA Benchmarks

  • < 8–10: Generally undervalued; worth investigating
  • 10–20: Fair to moderately valued for most sectors
  • > 20: Expensive; growth must justify the premium
  • Compare within sector — capital-intensive businesses (steel, cement) naturally have lower multiples than technology or FMCG

M.Cap / Sales

The Price-to-Sales ratio values the company relative to its revenue — useful when profits are negative or volatile. Lower is generally cheaper.

  • < 1: Very cheap on sales basis
  • 1–3: Moderate; depends on sector margins
  • > 5: Expensive; strong margin/growth needed

CMP / Book Value (Price-to-Book Ratio)

The Price-to-Book (P/B) ratio compares the market price of a share to its book value (net assets per share). Book value represents the accounting value of the company if it were liquidated — assets minus liabilities, divided by shares outstanding.

Formula

P/B = CMP ÷ Book Value Per Share

P/B < 1

Trading below book value — technically cheaper than the net asset worth. Could be a value opportunity, or the market is discounting poor asset quality or future losses. Common in banking sector.

P/B > 1

Market is paying a premium over book value — often because the company earns high returns on its assets (high ROE/ROCE). A high P/B with high ROCE is acceptable; a high P/B with low ROCE is a red flag.

Profitability Ratios

How efficiently does the company turn revenue and capital into profit?

ROCE and ROE

These two ratios measure how well a company converts the money invested in it into profits — ROCE considers all capital (equity + debt); ROE considers only shareholders' equity.

Formulas

ROCE % = (EBIT ÷ Capital Employed) × 100
ROE % = (Net Profit ÷ Shareholders' Equity) × 100
Capital Employed = Total Assets − Current Liabilities

ROCE Benchmarks
> 20%
Excellent. Company is generating very strong returns on invested capital. Often indicates a competitive moat. Ideal for long-term investors. Sustained >20% ROCE over 5+ years is rare and highly valuable.
15 – 20%
Good. Solid capital efficiency. Company is creating value above its cost of capital. Suitable for a quality-focused portfolio.
10 – 15%
Average. Company is generating returns but not spectacularly. Watch for trend — is ROCE improving or declining?
< 10%
Weak. Returns may not cover the cost of capital. Company may be destroying value. Avoid unless specific turnaround thesis.
ROCE vs ROE — Which Matters More?

Both matter — but ROCE is generally the better primary metric because it is not inflated by financial leverage. A company can show a high ROE simply by taking on more debt. ROCE, using capital employed (equity + debt), gives a cleaner picture of operating efficiency. For banks and NBFCs, ROE is more standard since their business model involves borrowing and lending.

Operating Profit Margin (OPM %)

The Operating Profit Margin measures what percentage of revenue remains as operating profit (EBIT or EBITDA) after deducting operating costs (raw materials, wages, overheads) but before interest and taxes. It reflects the core business profitability.

Formula

OPM % = (Operating Profit ÷ Net Sales) × 100

What to Look For

  • Stable or rising OPM over years = pricing power and cost control
  • Declining OPM = input cost pressures or competitive erosion
  • OPM Ann % vs OPM Prev Ann % — compare current year to previous year on annualised basis

Sector Context

  • FMCG/Consumer: 15–25% typical
  • IT Services: 20–35%
  • Auto/Manufacturing: 8–14%
  • Retail: 3–8%
  • Commodity: Highly variable, cyclical

Earnings Yield %

The Earnings Yield is simply the inverse of the P/E ratio — it tells you what percentage return you are getting in terms of earnings for every rupee you invest at the current market price. It allows direct comparison with bond yields and fixed-deposit rates.

Formula

Earnings Yield % = (EPS ÷ CMP) × 100 = (1 ÷ P/E) × 100

How to Use Earnings Yield

If the 10-year government bond yield is 7% and a stock offers an Earnings Yield of 5%, you are taking on equity risk for less than the risk-free rate — generally a red flag. If the earnings yield is 10%, you earn 3% over the risk-free rate purely from current earnings, before any growth. Higher earnings yield relative to risk-free rates = better value.

Interest Coverage Ratio

The Interest Coverage Ratio measures how many times the company can pay its annual interest expenses out of its operating profit (EBIT). It is the primary indicator of a company's ability to service its debt comfortably.

Formula

Interest Coverage = EBIT ÷ Interest Expense

Interpretation
> 5×
Very safe. Company earns 5× more than its interest obligation. Even a significant profit drop would not threaten debt repayment.
3 – 5×
Comfortable. Healthy coverage. Adequate buffer for earnings fluctuations without defaulting on interest.
1.5 – 3×
Adequate but thin. Limited margin of safety. A business downturn could bring the company close to difficulty in servicing debt.
< 1.5×
Danger zone. Operating earnings barely cover interest. Any setback in revenue or margins could lead to default or need for emergency refinancing.

Growth Ratios

How fast is the business growing its profits and revenues?

Profit Variation — Quarterly, Annual, 3-Year, 5-Year

Growth ratios track how company profits are changing over time — on a quarterly basis (to catch recent momentum), year-on-year (annual comparison), and over three- and five-year periods (to assess sustained compounding ability). Each has a distinct use.

Qtr Profit Var % — Quarterly Profit Variation

Percentage change in net profit for the most recent quarter compared to the same quarter in the previous year (Year-on-Year or YoY). This removes seasonality distortion — comparing Q1 FY26 to Q1 FY25, not to Q4 FY25.

Formula

Qtr Profit Var % = [(Current Quarter Profit − Same Quarter Last Year Profit) ÷ Same Quarter Last Year Profit] × 100

Example: Q1 FY25 profit = ₹120 Cr; Q1 FY24 = ₹100 Cr → Qtr Profit Var = 20% growth YoY.

Profit Growth % — Annual Profit Growth

Net profit growth for the most recently completed financial year compared to the prior year. Shows the overall annual trend in profitability.

Formula

Profit Growth % = [(FY24 Net Profit − FY23 Net Profit) ÷ FY23 Net Profit] × 100

Profit Var 3Yrs % and 5Yrs % — CAGR Over Multiple Years

Profit growth expressed as a Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) over the past 3 or 5 years. CAGR smooths out year-to-year volatility and shows the sustained compounding rate — the most important growth metric for long-term investors.

CAGR Formula

CAGR = [(End Value ÷ Start Value)^(1/n) − 1] × 100
where n = number of years

Example: Profit FY21 = ₹300 Cr; FY24 = ₹600 Cr → CAGR = [(600÷300)^(1/3) − 1] × 100 ≈ 26% per year.

Long-Term Investor Target

For long-term wealth creation, seek companies with Profit CAGR > 15% over 5 years — consistently, not just in boom years. A company growing profits at 20% annually doubles them in about 3.5 years. Compare profit growth with sales growth — profits growing faster than sales suggests margin expansion (excellent); profits growing slower than sales suggests margin compression (watch carefully).

Sales Variation — Quarterly, Annual, 3-Year, 5-Year

The same framework applies to revenue/sales growth: Qtr Sales Var % (latest quarter vs same quarter last year), Sales Growth % (annual YoY), and Sales Var 3Yrs % / 5Yrs % (CAGR). Revenue growth is the engine that drives long-term profit growth.

Healthy Signals

  • Sales growing consistently at 12%+ CAGR over 5 years
  • Sales growth leading or matching profit growth
  • Sales growth driven by volume expansion, not just price increases
  • Market share gaining in a growing industry

Warning Signs

  • Sales declining for 2+ consecutive quarters
  • Profits growing but sales stagnant — often unsustainable (cost-cutting phase)
  • Sales growth slowing rapidly over the 5-year trend
  • Revenue heavily dependent on one customer or region

Liquidity Ratios

Can the company meet its short-term obligations without external help?

Current Ratio, Quick Ratio, and Cash Ratio

These three ratios measure a company's ability to pay its short-term liabilities (obligations due within one year) using different tiers of its short-term assets. They form a spectrum from most lenient (Current Ratio) to most conservative (Cash Ratio).

Formulas

Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Quick Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities
Cash Ratio = (Cash + Cash Equivalents) ÷ Current Liabilities

Ratio What It Includes What It Excludes Ideal Range Strictness
Current Ratio Cash, Receivables, Inventory, Short-term investments Nothing (all current assets) 1.5 – 3.0 Least strict
Quick Ratio Cash, Receivables, Short-term investments Inventory (may take time to sell) 1.0 – 2.0 Stricter
Cash Ratio Cash and Cash Equivalents only Inventory and Receivables 0.2 – 0.5 Most strict

Healthy Signals

  • Current Ratio > 1.5 — comfortable short-term buffer
  • Quick Ratio > 1.0 — can meet liabilities without selling inventory
  • Both ratios improving or stable year-on-year

Warning Signs

  • Current Ratio < 1.0 — cannot cover short-term debts with existing assets
  • Current Ratio very high (>4) — assets sitting idle, poor capital deployment
  • Large gap between Current Ratio and Quick Ratio — huge inventory build-up

Leverage & Capital Ratios

How much debt does the company carry, and is it generating real cash or just accounting profits?

Debt / Equity Ratio

The Debt/Equity (D/E) ratio compares a company's total financial debt to its shareholders' equity. It shows how much of the business is funded by borrowed money versus owners' capital. Higher debt amplifies both profits and losses — beneficial in good times, dangerous in downturns.

Formula

Debt/Equity = Total Financial Debt ÷ Shareholders' Equity

D/E Interpretation (Non-Financial Companies)
0 – 0.5
Debt-free or near debt-free. Excellent financial safety. Company funded primarily by equity. Maximum resilience in downturns.
0.5 – 1.0
Moderate leverage. Acceptable for most industries. Company has more equity than debt. Manageable risk.
1.0 – 2.0
High leverage. More debt than equity. Acceptable in capital-intensive sectors (infrastructure, utilities) if interest coverage is strong.
> 2.0
Danger zone for most sectors. Very high financial risk. Any revenue shock can threaten solvency. Needs high and stable EBITDA to be safe.

CFO to PAT Ratio — Cash Flow Quality Test

The CFO to PAT (Cash Flow from Operations to Profit After Tax) ratio is one of the most important — yet often overlooked — measures of earnings quality. It compares actual cash generated from business operations to the profit reported on the income statement.

Formula

CFO to PAT = Cash Flow from Operations (CFO) ÷ Profit After Tax (PAT)

Interpretation
> 1.0
Excellent earnings quality. Company is generating more cash than its reported profit — strong and conservative accounting. Highly favoured by value investors.
0.7 – 1.0
Good. Earnings are substantially backed by real cash. Minor working capital differences are normal.
0.4 – 0.7
Caution. Significant portion of profits are not yet converted to cash. Could be due to rising receivables, inventory build-up, or aggressive revenue recognition.
< 0.4 or Negative
Red flag. Profits are not being converted to cash. Could indicate poor working capital management, aggressive accounting, or fictitious revenue. Investigate immediately.
The Earnings Quality Warning

A company can show impressive profits on paper while generating little or no cash — by recognising revenue early, delaying provisioning, or capitalising expenses. Cash does not lie. Consistently low CFO/PAT ratios have preceded many accounting frauds and corporate blow-ups. This ratio (also called the Accruals Test) is test #4 in the Piotroski F-Score for exactly this reason.

Working Capital Efficiency Ratios

How fast does money flow through the business? How long is cash tied up in operations?

Debtor Days (Receivable Days)

Debtor Days measures how long — on average — a company waits to collect payment from its customers after making a sale on credit. It directly affects cash flow: a business making large sales but collecting slowly can run out of cash even while showing profits.

Formula

Debtor Days = (Trade Receivables ÷ Net Sales) × 365

Lower = Better

  • Faster cash collection — less working capital needed
  • Less risk of bad debts and write-offs
  • FMCG/retail: 10–30 days (cash business)
  • Manufacturing/B2B: 45–90 days (normal)
  • Capital goods/projects: 90–180 days (can be acceptable)

Red Flags

  • Sharp jump in debtor days (e.g., 30 → 150) could mean forced credit extension to customers, disputed invoices, or deteriorating customer quality
  • Sustained high or rising debtor days without business justification
  • Profit growing while debtor days rise sharply — possible revenue manipulation

Inventory Days

Inventory Days measures how long raw materials, work-in-progress, and finished goods sit in stock before being sold. High inventory days tie up capital and increase the risk of obsolescence, spoilage, or write-downs.

Formula

Inventory Days = (Inventory ÷ Cost of Goods Sold) × 365

Context Matters

Inventory Days vary enormously by industry. A pharmaceutical company may hold 90–180 days of stock (long shelf life, regulatory requirements). A grocery retailer should hold 15–30 days. A specialty engineering company may have 200+ days due to long manufacturing cycles. Always compare to the company's own history and sector peers — not absolute numbers.

Days Payable

Days Payable measures how long the company takes to pay its suppliers. Companies that can delay supplier payments while collecting customer payments faster — are effectively using their supply chain to finance operations, reducing the need for working capital loans.

Formula

Days Payable = (Trade Payables ÷ Cost of Goods Sold) × 365

Higher Days Payable

  • Company uses supplier credit to fund operations
  • Indicates negotiating power with suppliers
  • Reduces need for working capital borrowings
  • Common in large retailers and strong-brand consumer companies

Watch Out For

  • Excessively high payable days (250+) can strain supplier relationships and supply chain reliability
  • Sudden drop in days payable while receivables rise = classic cash flow squeeze
  • Very low days payable when business is struggling may signal suppliers demanding faster payment

Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)

The Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC) is the single most comprehensive measure of a company's working capital efficiency. It measures the number of days between when a company pays for raw materials and when it receives cash from selling the finished product.

Formula

CCC = Debtor Days + Inventory Days − Days Payable

CCC Interpretation
Negative CCC
Exceptional. Company receives cash from customers BEFORE paying suppliers. Cash is self-financing. Common in retail giants (Amazon, Walmart, D-Mart). Extremely powerful position.
0 – 60 days
Efficient. Low working capital requirement. Business converts operations to cash quickly. Good for most consumer and service businesses.
60 – 120 days
Moderate. Acceptable for manufacturing and B2B businesses. Working capital loans may be needed to bridge the gap.
> 180 days
High working capital stress. Cash locked up for very long periods. May require significant external funding. Investigate cause — industry norm or operational inefficiency?

Working Capital Days

Working Capital Days expresses how many days of annual revenue are locked in net working capital (current assets minus current liabilities, excluding cash and short-term debt). It shows how capital-intensive the day-to-day operations are.

Formula

Working Capital Days = (Net Working Capital ÷ Revenue) × 365

Negative WC Days

Company's operations are funded by suppliers — cash-light model. The lower (more negative) the better. This is the hallmark of very powerful consumer businesses with strong brand recognition (FMCG, quick-service restaurants, grocery retail).

Positive WC Days

Company needs its own cash (or borrowed money) to fund daily operations. Acceptable in most B2B and manufacturing businesses. Higher values mean more capital is tied up — watch for deteriorating trend year-on-year.

Shareholding & Price Return Ratios

Who owns the business, how is confidence changing, and what returns has the stock delivered?

Pledged %, Promoter Holding, and Institutional Changes

Promoter Holding %

The percentage of shares held by the founders, promoter group, or controlling shareholders. Higher promoter holding typically aligns their interests with minority shareholders — they profit and suffer alongside other investors. A promoter holding consistently above 50% is considered strong alignment.

Pledged %

The percentage of promoter-held shares that have been pledged as collateral for loans taken by the promoters personally. This is a serious red flag — if promoters default on their personal loans, the lender can sell these shares in the open market, crashing the stock price without any fundamental business deterioration.

Change in Promoter / FII / DII Holding

  • Rising FII (Foreign Institutional Investor) holding: International funds becoming more confident — often a positive signal
  • Rising DII (Domestic Institutional Investor) holding: Indian mutual funds and insurance companies buying — also positive
  • Falling promoter holding: Founders reducing stake — investigate why. Could be estate planning, genuine liquidity need, or loss of faith in prospects
  • Promoter buying: One of the strongest insider confidence signals
Pledged % — The Most Dangerous Red Flag

A pledged % above 30% of promoter shares should trigger immediate caution. Above 50% is extremely dangerous. When the stock falls, pledge calls can cascade — lenders force-sell pledged shares, the stock falls further, triggering more calls in a destructive spiral. Many high-profile Indian corporate collapses (IL&FS, DHFL, YES Bank) had high pledge levels as early warning signs that were ignored.

Price Returns — 1 Week to 3 Years

Screeners show price returns over multiple horizons — 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 3 years. Each horizon serves a different purpose for investors.

Return PeriodWhat It Tells YouLong-Term Investor Use
1W / 1M return Very recent price momentum; reflects news, results, or market sentiment Least relevant for fundamental investors; may create opportunity if sharp fall is news-driven, not fundamental
3M / 6M return Short-term trend; reflects earnings season reactions or sector rotation Useful to identify recent underperformers (potential entry) or momentum stocks (potential exit)
1Yr return Annual performance; shows how the stock has done through a full earnings cycle Compare against index (Nifty, Sensex) and sector peers — significant underperformance warrants investigation
3Yr return Medium-term compounding; captures at least one business cycle Most meaningful for long-term investors — quality businesses should outperform the index over 3 years
The Down % Column

The Down % column in your screener shows how far the stock is from its 52-week or all-time high. A stock that is 30–50% down from its high is worth investigating — but not automatically buying. A large fall can mean the market is right (fundamentals have deteriorated) or an overreaction to temporary news. Check if ROCE, profit growth, and debt ratios have changed significantly before acting.

Key Red Flags and Long-Term Investment Checklist

Red Flags — When to Investigate Before Investing

Earnings Quality

  • CFO to PAT ratio consistently below 0.5 — profits not converting to cash
  • Debtor days rising sharply while profits also rise — possible aggressive revenue recognition
  • Piotroski Score below 3 — failing most financial health tests

Leverage and Solvency

  • Debt/Equity above 2.0 with falling ROCE — debt burden exceeding returns
  • Interest Coverage below 1.5× — earnings barely covering interest
  • Altman Z-Score below 1.81 — distress zone, possible bankruptcy risk
  • Pledged % above 30–40% of promoter shares

Operational Efficiency

  • CCC rising consistently over 3+ years — operational deterioration
  • Inventory Days doubling without demand justification
  • ROCE declining for 3+ consecutive years

Growth and Valuation

  • PEG above 3.0 with no clear growth acceleration catalyst
  • Profit growth decelerating while P/E remains elevated
  • Sales declining while stock trades at premium valuation

Ideal Long-Term Stock Profile

  • ROCE > 15% consistently over 5 years
  • Piotroski Score ≥ 7
  • Debt/Equity < 0.5 (preferably debt-free)
  • Interest Coverage > 5×
  • CFO to PAT > 0.7 (high earnings quality)
  • Pledged % = 0 or very low (<10%)
  • Profit CAGR > 15% over 5 years
  • CCC stable or improving
  • PEG between 0.5 – 1.5
  • Promoter holding > 50% with no recent reduction

How to Use Ratios Together

  • Start with ROCE and Profit CAGR — is the business fundamentally good?
  • Check Debt/Equity and Interest Coverage — is the balance sheet safe?
  • Assess CFO/PAT and Debtor Days — is profit quality real?
  • Screen for Piotroski Score ≥ 7 to narrow the list
  • Use PEG and EV/EBITDA to assess whether the price is reasonable
  • Check Pledged % and promoter changes last
  • Never decide on one ratio alone — always use the full picture

Composite Scoring Metrics

Single-number summary scores derived from multiple accounting tests

Piotroski F-Score (0–9)

Developed by Professor Joseph Piotroski of Stanford University, the F-Score assigns one point for each of nine accounting tests a company passes. Scores range from 0 (weakest) to 9 (strongest). It was designed specifically to separate genuinely improving companies from deteriorating ones among value stocks — businesses that look cheap on price-to-book but may be cheap for good reasons.

The Nine Tests — One Point Each

Category 1 — Profitability (4 Tests)

  • Net Income > 0: Company earned positive profits last year
  • Operating Cash Flow > 0: Business is actually generating cash (not just accounting profits)
  • ROA (Return on Assets) improving: Profitability per rupee of assets is better than last year
  • Cash Flow > Net Income (Accruals test): Cash earnings exceed accounting earnings — a sign of high-quality, manipulation-resistant profits

Category 2 — Leverage, Liquidity & Funding (3 Tests)

  • Debt-to-Equity improving: Less long-term debt relative to equity than last year (financial risk reducing)
  • Current Ratio improving: Short-term liquidity is better than last year
  • No new equity dilution: Company did not issue new shares (avoiding dilution of existing shareholders)

Category 3 — Operating Efficiency (2 Tests)

  • Gross Margin improving: Company earning a higher proportion of revenue after direct costs
  • Asset Turnover improving: Generating more sales per rupee of assets than last year
How to Interpret the F-Score
8 – 9
Excellent financial health. Company is improving across profitability, liquidity, and efficiency simultaneously. Strong buy candidate for value investors. Example: Balkrishna Industries (Score 8).
6 – 7
Good — fundamentally sound. Most tests passed. Company is stable and improving. Suitable for long-term portfolios. Example: Apollo Tyres (Score 7).
3 – 5
Average — needs caution. Mixed signals; some financial metrics improving, others declining. Investigate further. Example: MRF (Score 5).
0 – 2
Weak — high risk. Company is failing most financial health tests. Often a value trap — cheap for a reason. Avoid or exit. Example: JK Tyre (Score 4 — borderline).
Long-Term Investor Tip

Use the F-Score as a filter, not a trigger. A score of 8–9 does not mean "buy immediately" — it means the company is financially improving and worth deeper research. Combine with valuation ratios (is it priced reasonably?) and qualitative analysis (does it have a durable competitive advantage?).

Graham's Number

Created by Benjamin Graham — the father of value investing and Warren Buffett's mentor — Graham's Number estimates the maximum fair price a conservative investor should pay for a stock. It is grounded in two of Graham's key rules: P/E ≤ 15 and P/B ≤ 1.5 (multiplied together: 15 × 1.5 = 22.5).

Formula

Graham's Number = √ (22.5 × EPS × Book Value Per Share)

CMP ≤ Graham's Number

Stock is undervalued — trading below conservative fair value. A potential value buy according to Graham's method. Safety margin exists.

CMP > Graham's Number

Stock is overvalued — trading above what Graham would consider fair. Most growth stocks will exceed this number; it is a conservative benchmark, not the only valuation tool.

Important Limitation

Graham's Number works best for stable, asset-heavy businesses. For high-growth technology or service companies with low book value, it produces misleadingly low numbers. Most quality Indian stocks trade above Graham's Number — this does not automatically mean they are bad investments. Use Graham's Number as one data point, not the final verdict. Pair it with ROCE, earnings quality, and growth.

Altman Z-Score

The Altman Z-Score, developed by Professor Edward Altman in 1968, predicts the probability of a company going bankrupt within two years. It is widely used by credit analysts, institutional investors, and bond rating agencies. It combines five financial ratios into a single score.

Formula (for publicly listed manufacturing firms)

Z = 1.2×X₁ + 1.4×X₂ + 3.3×X₃ + 0.6×X₄ + 1.0×X₅

The Five Components

  • X₁ = Working Capital / Total Assets — measures short-term liquidity relative to size
  • X₂ = Retained Earnings / Total Assets — measures accumulated profitability (older, profitable firms score higher)
  • X₃ = EBIT / Total Assets — measures operating efficiency; how well assets generate operating profit
  • X₄ = Market Value of Equity / Total Liabilities — measures financial leverage from market's perspective
  • X₅ = Sales / Total Assets — measures asset utilisation / turnover
Z-Score Zones
Z > 2.99
Safe Zone. Low probability of bankruptcy. Company is financially stable.
1.81 – 2.99
Grey Zone. Some financial risk. Monitor closely. Does not guarantee bankruptcy but warrants caution.
Z < 1.81
Distress Zone. High probability of bankruptcy within two years. Avoid for investment; exit if held.

🎯 Summary — The Long-Term Investor's Ratio Framework

Financial ratios are not formulas to be memorised — they are questions in numerical form. Is the business profitable? (ROCE, OPM, EPS growth). Is it safe? (D/E, Interest Coverage, Current Ratio, Altman Z). Is it generating real cash? (CFO/PAT, Debtor Days, CCC). Is it priced reasonably? (P/E, PEG, EV/EBITDA, Graham's Number). Is management trustworthy? (Pledged %, Promoter holding changes, Piotroski accruals test).

No single ratio answers all these questions. The best investors use them together — triangulating from multiple angles before reaching a conclusion. A business that scores well across profitability, safety, cash quality, and valuation simultaneously is rare — and worth owning for years.
